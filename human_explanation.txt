Fantastic8 physics audit — student explanation

What the project is calculating
--------------------------------

The three audited Python modules describe a particle confined to a two-
dimensional shape: a rectangle (including a square), a circle, or an
equilateral triangle. The code calculates stationary quantum states. A
stationary state has a definite energy and a spatial wavefunction. These
modules do not build the paper's moving Gaussian wave packets or calculate
their later-time revivals.

The supplied paper is primarily about Gaussian wave packets moving in square,
triangle, and circular billiards. It gives exact stationary-state formulas
that can be used as building blocks. The code focuses on those stationary
formulas rather than reproducing the paper's full time-dependent examples.

Schrödinger equation and infinite walls
--------------------------------------

The time-independent Schrödinger equation is the quantum equation that relates
a particle's wavefunction to its energy and the potential energy in each
location. In an infinite-well problem, the potential is zero inside the
allowed shape and infinitely high outside it. A particle in the model cannot
exist outside the boundary.

At an infinite wall, the wavefunction must be zero. For example, the
rectangle's sine factors become zero on each edge. In the circle, the Bessel
function is chosen at a root so the wavefunction is zero at the circular wall.
The triangle uses a sum of sine/cosine terms chosen to vanish on the three
sides.

The code does not numerically solve the two-dimensional Schrödinger PDE on a
mesh. The rectangle and triangle use analytical trigonometric formulas; the
circle uses a Bessel function and numerical Bessel roots. A grid or mesh in
these modules is mainly for evaluating or drawing the already-defined
wavefunction.

Energy levels and quantum numbers
---------------------------------

An energy eigenvalue is one of the allowed energies for the confined
particle. Quantum numbers label which state is being described. Their meaning
depends on the shape:

* Rectangle: `m` and `n` are positive sine-mode indices in the x and y
  directions.
* Circle: `m` is angular order and `n` is the radial root number in the code.
* Triangle: `m` and `n` are labels for combinations of six plane waves.

The paper and code do not always use the same labels. In the paper's triangle
section the labels `(M,N)` must satisfy `M >= 2N`. The module uses a
transformed pair `(m,n)` with `m >= n`; the mapping is `M=m+n`, `N=n`.
For example, the paper state `(M,N)=(3,1)` is module input `(m,n)=(2,1)`.
The code's triangle labels should not be compared numerically to the paper's
labels without this conversion.

For the circle, the paper numbers radial nodes starting at `n_r=0`; the code
numbers its first positive Bessel root as `n=1`. Thus paper `(m,n_r)=(0,0)`
corresponds to code `(m,n)=(0,1)`.

The square and rectangle
------------------------

In `shapes/rectangle.py`, `energy()` calculates the separated sine-well
energy. `psi()` computes the product of one sine function in x and one in y.
`probability_density()` computes the squared magnitude of that wavefunction.
The energy formula and wavefunction are exact analytical expressions.

The paper explicitly gives the square with equal sides `a x a`. The code also
accepts unequal side lengths `a` and `b`; that general-rectangle formula is
the standard separated-variable extension, not a fully worked result in the
paper. In numerical checks, the tested square energies agreed with the
paper's formula, and the unequal 1-by-2 rectangle agreed with the
independently derived extension.

The code uses SI Planck and electron-mass constants directly. Therefore the
raw energy from this module is in joules when lengths are in metres. The
paper's sample values use a dimensionless convention. To compare numbers
directly, one must use consistent units or divide out the appropriate energy
scale.

The circle
----------

In `shapes/circle.py`, `_lowest_states()` selects roots of Bessel functions,
`_psi()` evaluates the radial Bessel part and angular sine or cosine part,
and `_normalization()` sets the total probability integral to one. The radial
integral uses the polar-area factor `r`; omitting that factor would give the
wrong normalization.

The paper uses complex angular waves with positive and negative angular
momentum. The code displays real cosine and sine modes. For nonzero angular
order those are valid combinations of the paper's two equal-energy states.
The tested first roots and second zero state had matching energies,
normalization, boundary values, and wavefunctions after accounting for the
radial-index shift.

The triangle
------------

In `shapes/equilateral_triangle.py`, `k_vectors()` constructs the six wave
vectors; `wavefunction()` adds their signed sine or cosine values and applies
a normalization factor. `run_triangle()` validates the indices, computes an
energy coefficient, and writes PNG plots.

The code's triangle is drawn with its base along the bottom, while the paper
places the base at the top. They are the same triangle after a rotation/
reflection and translation. To compare the coordinate values, use
`x_paper=x_module-a/2` and `y_paper=sqrt(3)*a/2-y_module`.

After applying both the coordinate conversion and the index conversion, the
three tested triangle states agreed with the formulas in the paper. For the
non-degenerate state, the calculated wavefunction had the opposite overall
sign from the paper's formula; multiplying a wavefunction by -1 does not
change its physical probability density or energy. For two degenerate tests,
the code's states belonged to the paper's two-dimensional degenerate
eigenspaces; they were compared to the whole space rather than requiring an
arbitrary choice of one basis function.

One integration limitation is important: `run_triangle()` does not return a
numeric physical energy. It returns an energy coefficient and equation text.
The adapter elsewhere in the project computes an SI energy, but the triangle
module's own returned value should not be described as a joule-valued energy.

Wavefunction, probability, and normalization
-------------------------------------------

The wavefunction `psi` is a probability-amplitude function. It can be
positive or negative (or complex). Probability density is `|psi|^2`, so it
cannot be negative. The rectangle and circle modules explicitly use the
squared magnitude; the triangle's current wavefunctions are real, so
`psi**2` is the same as `|psi|^2`.

Normalization means that the total probability over the physical shape is
one:

    integral over shape of |psi|^2 dA = 1

This is why the numerical normalization integrals in the test table should
be close to 1. For the circle, `dA = r dr dtheta`; the extra `r` is necessary
because a thin ring farther from the center covers more area than a ring of
the same thickness near the center.

How to read the test results
----------------------------

`test_results.csv` lists 10 executed cases. `PASS` means the energy or energy
factor, normalization, sampled boundary value, and applicable wavefunction
comparison met the explicit test thresholds listed in the technical report.
It does not mean every possible state or every project feature has been
tested.

* Absolute error is the size of the difference between the reference and the
  module's value.
* Relative error is absolute error divided by the reference magnitude. For
  example, if the reference is 10 and the result is 10.1, the relative error
  is 0.1/10 = 0.01, or 1%.
* Normalization error is how far the integrated probability is from 1.
* Boundary residual is the sampled nonzero wavefunction at a wall, or for the
  circle the Bessel-root residual that controls the wall value.
* Wavefunction relative L2 error compares whole fields over the domain. The
  comparison allows a global sign difference. For degenerate states it checks
  whether the state lies in the correct equal-energy subspace.

In the triangle rows, the scalar compared is the code's reduced energy
factor, not a full energy. The full scalar energy is not returned by the
triangle module. The circle decimal roots were found by an independent
bracketed root calculation; the paper does not list those exact root decimals.

What is correct, what differs, and what remains uncertain
---------------------------------------------------------

What matched in the tested cases:

* Rectangle square formulas, normalized sine products, density, and walls.
* Circle Bessel roots, energy equation, radial normalization, real angular
  states, and wall behavior after index conversion.
* Triangle energy coefficients, normalization, wall behavior, and
  wavefunction/eigenspace comparisons after index and coordinate conversion.

Differences or limitations to tell teammates:

1. The repository has stationary solvers, not a reproduction of the paper's
   complete moving-wave-packet and revival study.
2. The paper's triangle and circle labels need explicit mapping to code input
   labels.
3. The rectangle uses fixed SI constants; the circle defaults to dimensionless
   constants; and the triangle returns a reduced energy factor. Do not compare
   raw values across shapes without a common unit convention.
4. The triangle's own result lacks numeric arrays and a numeric physical
   energy; the circle can return Python figure objects when plotting is
   enabled; and no shape currently returns a potential grid.
5. Only selected low states were tested. Higher modes, orthogonality across
   states, broad convergence sweeps, UI behavior, and all plot pixels remain
   untested.
6. The supplied PDF confirms the arXiv preprint title, authors, and date, but
   does not expose the journal citation or DOI. Those details were not
   independently confirmed from this file.

The tests did not edit any project source. Triangle PNGs produced by the
calculation were written only under the audit artifact folder. The technical
report documents two temporary files created outside that folder during PDF
inspection and isolated plotting; no packages were installed and no Git
history-changing operation was performed.
